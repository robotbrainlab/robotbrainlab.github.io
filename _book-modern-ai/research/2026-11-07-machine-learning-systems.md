## Overview

**WHAT THIS AREA IS**

The machine, and the software that exists only because of the machine: how a model is split across devices, how a kernel is written against a memory hierarchy, how requests are scheduled and memory reclaimed while a model serves, what numeric format the hardware multiplies in, and how a cluster keeps making progress while parts of it fail. The object of study is the system that runs a model, not the model. Just outside: the architecture or objective being run (the method areas), the data pipeline feeding it, operations tooling and model registries, compute-management interfaces, and benchmark design.

## Distributed training parallelism

**NAME** — Distributed training parallelism. Also: model parallelism; sharded data parallelism (ZeRO, FSDP); tensor, pipeline, context/sequence and expert parallelism; "N-D parallelism".

**WHY IT MATTERS** — A model whose parameters, gradients and optimiser states do not fit in one device's memory cannot be trained on one device, and a model that does fit still trains too slowly to be useful. Parallelism is how the work is cut up, and the cut determines how much must be communicated per step. It is receiving attention now for two reasons visible in instrument B: clusters are no longer uniform (MLSys 2026 has papers on heterogeneous GPU clusters and geo-distributed training), and long context has made the sequence dimension itself a thing to shard, which the older data/tensor/pipeline taxonomy did not cover.

**FOUNDATIONS** — No single canonical reference; three stand in for one. ZeRO (Rajbhandari et al., arXiv:1910.02054, Oct 2019) — partition optimiser state, gradients and parameters across data-parallel ranks instead of replicating them; its abstract claims models "over 100B parameter" with "super-linear speedup on 400 GPUs" and "throughput of 15 Petaflops", naming no baseline for the throughput figure. Megatron-LM (arXiv:1909.08053) for intra-layer tensor parallelism and GPipe (arXiv:1811.06965) for pipeline scheduling and the bubble — I did not fetch either in this session. Underneath all three is one physical constraint that needs no citation: synchronous SGD requires the gradient to be reduced across all ranks every step, so communication volume scales with parameter count and the step time is bounded below by the slowest link in the collective.

**LIVE RESEARCH** — Open: how to shard the sequence dimension without wasting work on short sequences, and how to train on clusters that are not uniform. Dated items, all from instrument B (MLSys 2026, May 2026) unless stated. **FCP** (<https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/e01c431bbb83153632c0dcfaf8ccda0a-Abstract-Conference.html>) argues existing context parallelism over-shards short sequences and fails to bin-pack mixed-length ones; it shards at block granularity with arbitrary peer-to-peer communication instead of a ring, reporting attention MFU 1.13×–2.21× better and near-linear scaling to 256 NVIDIA GPUs, **naming no baseline system** — only "existing CP designs". **veScale-FSDP** ("Flexible and High-Performance FSDP at Scale") and **HexiScale** and **Zorse** (training over heterogeneous hardware / heterogeneous GPU clusters) are in the same proceedings. From instrument A, October 2026: "GPU-Initiated Communication: Dissecting Down to the Bone" and "MegaFlux: Skew-Resilient MoE Megakernels via Pipelined Expert Replication". The question has not settled; the number of parallelism dimensions has grown rather than converged.

**TRANSITION** — Established engineering: sharded data parallelism and tensor parallelism. Evidence that it is practice and not proposal — instrument D requires FSDP-class and Megatron-class experience of a senior hire, and ByteDance published its own production FSDP at MLSys 2026. Becoming practice: context/sequence parallelism, which now has competing designs rather than one, and hybrid parallelism chosen by a planner rather than by hand (FlexTrain, NEST in instrument B). Still experimental: training across heterogeneous or geo-distributed hardware with partial synchronisation — each of HexiScale, Zorse and DreamDDP is a single group's system, with no evidence in what I read of independent replication.

**ENGINEERING** — In practice: PyTorch FSDP or DeepSpeed ZeRO for sharded data parallelism, Megatron-LM or MaxText/AXLearn for tensor and pipeline splits, NCCL (or MPI/UCX) underneath for the collectives — all named as requirements in instrument D. The central trade-off is memory against communication: every step you take toward holding less per device adds traffic to the interconnect, and the optimal point moves with the ratio of device FLOP/s to link bandwidth, so a plan tuned for one cluster is wrong on the next. The second trade-off is bubbles: pipeline stages idle, and the fix (more micro-batches) raises activation memory.

**SKILLS** — Able to compute, for a given model and cluster, the bytes communicated per step under each sharding scheme and compare that to the measured collective bandwidth. Able to read a memory budget: parameters, gradients, optimiser moments, activations, and what recomputation trades. Able to write and debug a ring all-reduce, and to reason about why a ring is bandwidth-optimal but latency-poor. Able to profile a distributed step and attribute time to compute, communication and exposed bubble. Practical experience: launching a multi-process job, diagnosing a hang in a collective, and recognising that one slow rank sets the step time.

**BUILDABLE** —
(a) FCP's result needs **256 NVIDIA GPUs** with high-bandwidth interconnect, a long-context corpus (terabytes of disk), and days of wall clock; ZeRO's needs 400. Cost: I decline to price 256 GPU-days rather than invent a rate I did not verify. **None of it exists on this machine.**
(b) Smallest experiment, and it fits: MLX ships `mpi` and `ring` backends with `all_sum`, `all_gather`, `sum_scatter`, and ranks can be processes on one host (instrument F). Implement optimiser-state sharding by hand on a sub-billion-parameter model; measure peak resident memory per rank at each ZeRO stage and count the bytes each stage all-gathers per step. Then time `all_sum` against message size. That measures the trade-off itself; it needs one Mac and no API spend. Tensor parallelism across two Macs needs MLX's JACCL backend — Thunderbolt cabling, full mesh, macOS 26.2 (instrument F) — out of reach with one machine.
HARDWARE: one Apple Silicon Mac suffices for the sharding and collective-cost experiment; the published results are not reproducible without a GPU cluster, and that is the honest finding.

## Memory-hierarchy-aware kernels and the attention kernel lineage

**NAME** — GPU kernel optimisation; IO-aware kernels; FlashAttention and successors; tile-based kernel DSLs (ThunderKittens, HipKittens, CuTe-DSL).

**WHY IT MATTERS** — Accelerator arithmetic throughput has grown faster than the bandwidth and capacity of the memory nearest the arithmetic units, so most kernels are limited by data movement rather than by multiplication. A kernel that keeps a tile resident and recomputes cheaply beats one that writes an intermediate out and reads it back, even when it does more arithmetic. Attention is the case where this mattered most, because the naive form materialises an N×N matrix. It is live now because each hardware generation shifts the bottleneck: the FlashAttention-4 authors state that on NVIDIA Blackwell, tensor-core throughput doubled while shared-memory bandwidth and the exponential units did not, which invalidates kernel designs tuned for the previous generation.

**FOUNDATIONS** — Two references stand in for one. FlashAttention (Dao et al., arXiv:2205.14135, May 2022) — exact attention computed tile-wise with online softmax so the N×N score matrix is never written to main memory; its abstract claims 15% end-to-end speedup on BERT-large against the MLPerf 1.1 record, 3× on GPT-2 and 2.4× on long-range arena **without naming baselines for the latter two**, and reports the first better-than-chance Transformer results on Path-X (16K) and Path-256 (64K). Under it sits the roofline model (Williams, Waterman and Patterson, 2009) — performance is bounded by min(peak FLOP/s, arithmetic intensity × bandwidth) — which I did not fetch in this session. The physical constraint is a hierarchy: registers, then shared memory/SRAM, then HBM, each roughly an order of magnitude slower and larger than the last.

**LIVE RESEARCH** — Open: how to keep hand-written kernel performance while hardware diverges, both between generations and between vendors. **FlashAttention-4** (MLSys 2026, <https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/ae8b0b5838ba510daff1198474e7b984-Abstract-Conference.html>): redesigned asynchronous pipelines with larger tiles, software-emulated exponentials, conditional softmax rescaling, and 2-CTA MMA to cut shared-memory traffic; reports up to 1.3× over cuDNN 9.13, 2.7× over Triton, and 1613 TFLOP/s on B200 described as 71% utilisation, with compile times 20–30× faster than C++ templates because it is written in CuTe-DSL from Python. **HipKittens** (MLSys 2026, <https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/bc75fa9843a7905bbed9d83895a88f7f-Abstract-Conference.html>): tile primitives port from NVIDIA to AMD CDNA3/CDNA4 but their implementing algorithms must be redesigned; competitive with AMD's hand-written assembly, 1.2–2.4× over all available baselines in specific settings (d=64 attention, GQA non-causal backward, memory-bound kernels), productionised in AITER. Also at MLSys 2026: ParallelKittens (multi-GPU kernels), Flashlight (PyTorch compiler extensions for attention variants), BLASST (blocked attention sparsity). The answer is moving every hardware generation.

**TRANSITION** — Established engineering: tiled, IO-aware attention is the default path in production inference and training stacks; the evidence is that cuDNN and Triton are the *baselines* FlashAttention-4 is measured against, not the frontier. Becoming practice: writing peak kernels in an embedded Python DSL rather than C++ templates or assembly — CuTe-DSL in FlashAttention-4, and HipKittens' claim of having been productionised in AMD's AITER, both single-group evidence. Still experimental: portable tile abstractions that reach hand-assembly performance across vendors; HipKittens' own framing — that peak AMD kernels are "typically hand-written in raw assembly" — is the admission that portability is not yet achieved.

**ENGINEERING** — Built by choosing a tile shape that fits the fastest memory, overlapping loads with arithmetic through asynchronous copies, and trading recomputation for traffic. Tools in real use: CUDA/CUTLASS and CuTe, Triton, ThunderKittens and HipKittens, AMD AITER, with Nsight-class profilers to read achieved bandwidth and occupancy. The central trade-off is arithmetic for memory traffic — FlashAttention recomputes the softmax normalisation rather than storing it — and the second is portability for peak: every technique in FlashAttention-4 is specific to one GPU generation's asymmetry.

**SKILLS** — Able to compute a kernel's arithmetic intensity and place it on a roofline, and to say from that alone whether a change can possibly help. Able to derive the online (streaming) softmax recurrence and show it is numerically equivalent to the batched form. Able to read an occupancy and bandwidth report and decide whether a kernel is latency-, bandwidth- or compute-bound. Able to write a tiled matmul and attention kernel in at least one kernel language. Practical experience: correctness-testing a kernel against a reference in float64, and benchmarking honestly — warm-up, clock locking, repeated runs.

**BUILDABLE** —
(a) FlashAttention-4's headline needs a **B200 or GB200** and the CUDA/CuTe toolchain: unavailable here. Memory: a Blackwell HBM budget I did not verify at source. Disk: tens of gigabytes for the toolchain — my estimate, not a measurement. Time: the paper claims compiles 20–30× faster than template C++, so iteration is minutes. Cost: a day's B200 rental, which I did not price.
(b) Smallest experiment, and it fits: in NumPy or MLX, implement attention twice — materialising the full N×N scores, then tiled with the online-softmax recurrence — and plot peak memory and wall time against sequence length from 512 to 32768. The first grows quadratically and fails at some N; the second grows linearly. Then measure achieved GB/s for a large elementwise op and a large matmul, and locate the roofline knee. It cannot teach the asynchronous pipelining and tensor-memory work the 2026 papers are about.
HARDWARE: Apple Silicon suffices for tiling and roofline; the 2026 kernel results need NVIDIA Blackwell or AMD CDNA and are not reproducible on it.

## Inference serving: KV-cache memory management and request scheduling

**NAME** — LLM inference serving systems; KV-cache management; paged attention; continuous (iteration-level) batching; chunked prefill; prefill/decode disaggregation.

**WHY IT MATTERS** — Autoregressive decoding is memory-bound and stateful: each sequence carries a key/value cache that grows per token, and the cache, not the weights, usually limits how many requests can share a device. How that memory is allocated and how requests are batched and scheduled decides throughput and tail latency more than any kernel does. It is live now because the workload keeps changing shape — reasoning traces make decode far longer, agents make prefix reuse common, and Mixture-of-Experts models change the cost of each phase — so scheduling policies tuned to 2023 chat traffic no longer hold.

**FOUNDATIONS** — No single canonical reference; two carry most of it. PagedAttention/vLLM (Kwon et al., arXiv:2309.06180, Sep 2023, SOSP 2023) — treat the KV cache as fixed-size blocks with an indirection table, as an operating system pages memory, so fragmentation and duplication go away; it claims 2–4× throughput over FasterTransformer and Orca at equal latency and "near-zero waste in KV cache memory", and notably gives **no percentage** for the waste in prior systems. Orca (OSDI 2022) introduced iteration-level scheduling — batching at the granularity of a decode step rather than a request (not fetched here). Beneath both is one arithmetic fact: a decode step reads all weights to produce one token per sequence, so arithmetic intensity rises with batch size, which is why batching is the whole game.

**LIVE RESEARCH** — Open: when to split prefill from decode, and how to manage a cache under sparsity, quantisation and reuse. Dated items from instrument B (MLSys 2026): **"Beyond the Buzz: A Pragmatic Take on Inference Disaggregation"** (<https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/d49cee5f3a79d97d719df255689d83d7-Abstract-Conference.html>), an NVIDIA-authored study of "hundreds of thousands of design points" concluding that disaggregation helps mainly for prefill-heavy traffic and larger models and depends on dynamic rate matching and elastic scaling — a notably conditional claim, with **no numbers and no named baseline system** in its abstract, and it names the authors' own product, Dynamo. **"Demystifying the Mixture of Experts Serving Tax"** (<https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/42a452cbafa9dd64e9ba4aa95cc1ef21-Abstract-Conference.html>, Patel et al.) reports MoE models serving 2–3× worse than FLOP-equivalent dense models, and that expert load imbalance hurts prefill while *helping* decode. Also in the same proceedings: OPKV, SkipKV, FlexiCache, Kitty (2-bit KV quantisation), PLA-Serve, layered prefill for MoE, GhostServe, DriftBench. From instrument A, October 2026: "ePACT: Energy-Performance-Aware Commitment Tracking for LLM Serving". This is the most active cluster I found: roughly 37 of the 118 MLSys 2026 titles I read.

**TRANSITION** — Established engineering: paged KV cache and continuous batching. Evidence beyond the paper — vLLM has 96.5k stars, 24.5k forks and "over 2000 contributors" (instrument E), and instrument D lists vLLM and SGLang among a senior engineer's required tools. Becoming practice: prefill/decode disaggregation — shipped in a vendor product (Dynamo) but the 2026 study of it is still establishing *when* it wins, which is the signature of something not yet settled. Still experimental: aggressive KV sparsification and 2-bit cache quantisation, where the accuracy cost is model- and task-dependent and each paper is a single group's.

**ENGINEERING** — Built as a scheduler over a block allocator: admission control, a running batch mutated every iteration, a block table per sequence with copy-on-write for shared prefixes, and preemption by recompute or swap when memory runs short. Real tools: vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, NVIDIA Dynamo. The central trade-off is throughput against tail latency — a larger running batch raises tokens per second and lengthens every individual request's decode, and prefill chunks that fill the device starve decode steps of their turn. Disaggregation buys phase isolation at the price of shipping the KV cache over the network.

**SKILLS** — Able to compute the KV cache bytes per token for a given model (layers × heads × head-dim × 2 × dtype) and from that the maximum concurrency on a given memory budget. Able to distinguish compute-bound prefill from memory-bound decode and predict which metric a change moves. Able to read a latency distribution and reason about time-to-first-token versus inter-token latency under an SLO. Able to implement a block allocator with reference counting and prefix sharing. Practical experience: load-testing a server with a realistic arrival process rather than a fixed batch, and seeing a cache-thrashing collapse.

**BUILDABLE** —
(a) vLLM's 2–4× needs an NVIDIA GPU with HBM for a 13B-class model and above, the baselines it compares against, and real traces. Memory: a datacentre-GPU budget. Disk: tens of gigabytes of weights per model compared. Time: hours per sweep. Cost: GPU rental I did not price. Not reproducible as published — though vLLM's README lists an Apple Silicon plugin, so a degraded single-device run is possible.
(b) Smallest experiment, and it fits: run a small local model with explicit control of its KV cache, and implement the allocator twice — contiguous per-sequence pre-allocation sized to the maximum length, then fixed-size blocks with a table. Draw output lengths from a skewed distribution; measure concurrent sequences per gigabyte and internal fragmentation under each. Then add iteration-level batching and compare tokens per second with static batching at one arrival rate. A hosted API is useless here: the cache is exactly what it hides.
HARDWARE: Apple Silicon suffices for the allocator and batching experiment; the published throughput comparisons need datacentre GPUs.

## Machine learning compilers and intermediate representations

**NAME** — ML compilers; deep-learning compilers; graph and kernel compilers; XLA, TVM, MLIR, Triton, torch.compile; kernel DSLs and autotuning.

**WHY IT MATTERS** — A framework describes a computation; a compiler decides how it runs on one specific device. Fusing operators removes round trips to main memory, choosing tile sizes and layouts decides whether matrix units are fed, and doing this by hand for every operator × shape × device is not possible — the space is too large and each new accelerator resets it. It is live now because the number of targets has grown (NVIDIA, AMD, TPU, and a list of NPUs that vLLM alone enumerates), because kernel DSLs have moved into Python, and because 2026 work is pointing language models at the autotuning problem itself.

**FOUNDATIONS** — No single canonical reference; three stand in. Halide (Ragan-Kelley et al., 2013) for separating the algorithm from the schedule, the idea every later system inherits; TVM (Chen et al., OSDI 2018) for learned cost models and automatic schedule search; MLIR (Lattner et al., 2020) for multi-level dialects so a graph can be lowered through several abstractions rather than one. I did not fetch any of the three in this session — treat their contents here as my own recollection, not as confirmed. The constraint underneath is the same roofline fact as for kernels: fusion is valuable because memory traffic, not arithmetic, is usually the limit, so the compiler's main job is to decide what stays in fast memory.

**LIVE RESEARCH** — Open: who writes the schedule — a human, a search, or a model — and how to compile for hardware the compiler's authors have never seen. Dated items, all MLSys 2026 (instrument B, May 2026). **Wave** (<https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/48c34730ff9a8574481a00ce8cb5e2cb-Abstract-Conference.html>), a Python-embedded symbolic DSL and compiler that automates the address arithmetic matrix cores require; its abstract claims it "matches or surpasses the performance of state-of-the-art kernel DSLs and libraries" while **naming no hardware, no baseline and no measurement** — an unusually bare claim for a performance paper. **CATWILD**, compiler autotuning for TPU workloads in the wild; **AccelOpt**, a self-improving LLM agentic system for accelerator kernel optimisation; **ApproxMLIR**, an accuracy-aware compiler; **Flashlight**, PyTorch compiler extensions for attention variants; **Agentic Operator Generation for ML ASICs**; "Dataflow Is All You Need" (titles confirmed at <https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026>). I classify about 15 of the 118 titles I read as kernel-and-compiler work. The striking 2026 shift is the number of papers putting a language model inside the compiler's search loop.

**TRANSITION** — Established engineering: graph capture and operator fusion in a production framework — instrument D names XLA and torch.compile as things a senior engineer is expected to know, and TPU work at Google is compiled through XLA as a matter of course. Becoming practice: writing kernels in a Python-embedded DSL instead of C++ templates, with FlashAttention-4's CuTe-DSL implementation and HipKittens as the evidence that peak kernels now come out of such a DSL. Still experimental: LLM-driven autotuning and agentic kernel generation — four MLSys 2026 papers, each a single group, no independent replication in anything I read, and no shared benchmark beyond one paper's own proposal of one (FlashInfer-Bench).

**ENGINEERING** — Built as a lowering pipeline: trace to a graph IR, fuse and rewrite, tile and schedule to a hardware IR, emit. Real tools: torch.compile with Inductor, XLA for TPU and GPU, Triton, MLIR-based stacks, TVM, and now CuTe-DSL and Wave at the kernel level. The central trade-off is generality against peak: a compiler that handles every operator leaves performance on the table for the five that matter, which is why production stacks hand the hot kernels to a library and compile the rest. The second is compile time against run time, which autotuning spends freely and which FlashAttention-4 treats as a first-class metric.

**SKILLS** — Able to read an IR dump and say which operators were fused and why the rest were not. Able to explain the algorithm/schedule separation and write two schedules for one algorithm. Able to reason about a search space: what the tunable knobs are (tile shape, unroll, layout, pipeline depth) and why exhaustive search is infeasible. Able to measure whether a fusion helped, rather than assuming it did. Practical experience: making a model compile at all — graph breaks, dynamic shapes, recompilation storms — and knowing when the compiler should be bypassed for a hand-written kernel.

**BUILDABLE** —
(a) The 2026 results need their targets: CATWILD Google TPUs and a production workload corpus, Wave and AccelOpt matrix-core GPUs. Memory and disk are modest — a toolchain and some models, tens of gigabytes, my estimate not a measurement — but the device is the blocker. Time: autotuning sweeps run hours to days. Cost: agentic search bills a model per candidate kernel, which on a paid API would dominate, and which I cannot price without the paper's token counts.
(b) Smallest experiment, and it fits: take one small transformer block, run it eagerly then compiled, and measure wall time, fused regions or kernel launches, and peak memory. Then break the fusion — a device round trip, a data-dependent branch — and watch the gain vanish; the benefit is memory traffic. Read the generated code. Caveat: which compiler paths work on Apple Silicon I did **not** verify at a source this session.
HARDWARE: one Mac is enough to see fusion appear and disappear and to read generated code; nothing in the 2026 autotuning literature is reproducible on it.

## Low-precision numerics as a hardware–software contract

**NAME** — Low-precision training and inference; mixed precision; FP8, FP4 (NVFP4, MXFP4), INT8/INT4; block scaling; quantisation-aware training.

**WHY IT MATTERS** — Every halving of the bits per value halves the bytes moved and, on hardware with matching matrix units, roughly doubles the arithmetic available. Since this area's binding constraint is memory traffic, numeric format is the cheapest large lever there is — and the only one that can silently change what the model computes. It is live now because hardware has started shipping 4-bit matrix formats and block-scaled types, which moves a question that used to be a software choice into a hardware–software contract: the format, the block size and where the scale lives are fixed in silicon, and the training recipe must be built around them.

**FOUNDATIONS** — No single canonical reference; three stand in. Mixed-precision training (Micikevicius et al., ICLR 2018) — keep a master copy of weights in higher precision and scale the loss so gradients do not underflow. The FP8 formats paper (Micikevicius et al., 2022) for E4M3/E5M2 and why two exponent/mantissa splits are needed for forward and backward. The Open Compute Project MX specification (2023) for block-scaled types, where a group of elements shares one exponent. I did not fetch any of the three in this session; treat them as recollection. The constraint is arithmetic, not convention: a floating-point format's dynamic range is set by its exponent bits, so an outlier beyond the range is lost, and the only defences are a scale factor and a smaller block over which that scale applies.

**LIVE RESEARCH** — Open: how low you can go in *training*, and how to keep a tensor in a low format end-to-end instead of casting around every operator. Dated items from instrument B (MLSys 2026, May 2026). **FP8-Flow-MoE** (<https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/9cb295d4dce6db99f01e0dd512e46ba0-Abstract-Conference.html>): current FP8 recipes convert back and forth to BF16 repeatedly, and keeping data in FP8 instead exposes "double quantisation error", where a tensor quantised along one dimension and then another carries mismatched scales; their casting-free dataflow with a scaling-aware transpose cuts explicit casts from 12 to 2, reporting up to 21% higher throughput and 16.5 GB less memory per GPU than BF16 and naive FP8 on a 671B MoE model — while **naming no GPU type at all**. Also at MLSys 2026: **MixLLM** (global mixed precision between output features), **Kitty** (2-bit KV cache with dynamic channel-wise precision boost), **CAGE** (curvature-aware gradient estimation for quantisation-aware training), **IntAttention** (a fully integer attention pipeline for edge inference). Instrument D lists NVFP4, MXFP4, FP8 and INT8 as named technologies for one role, which tells you 4-bit formats are in the hiring requirements, not just the papers.

**TRANSITION** — Established engineering: BF16/FP16 mixed-precision training with a higher-precision master weight, and INT8/4-bit weight-only quantisation for inference. Evidence: these are the assumed baselines that 2026 papers measure against, and 4-bit local inference is what consumer tooling ships. Becoming practice: FP8 training at scale — TransformerEngine and Megatron-LM integration is what FP8-Flow-MoE plugs into, so the plumbing exists, but the recipe is still being argued over, as a paper whose contribution is "stop casting" demonstrates. Still experimental: FP4 training, and aggressive sub-4-bit KV cache; I found these named in a hiring list and in single-group papers, and no independent replication in anything I read.

**ENGINEERING** — Built as a per-tensor or per-block scaling policy plus a cast schedule: decide which operators run in the low format, where scales are computed (delayed vs just-in-time), and which tensors stay high (usually accumulations, normalisations and the master weights). Real tools: NVIDIA TransformerEngine, Megatron-LM, bitsandbytes, llama.cpp and MLX quantisation for local inference. The central trade-off is range against resolution — fewer exponent bits give finer steps but lose outliers, and smaller blocks recover range at the cost of storing more scales. The second, which FP8-Flow-MoE is about, is that casting between formats can cost more than the low format saves.

**SKILLS** — Able to write out a format's representable range and step size from its exponent and mantissa bits, and predict which tensors in a transformer will overflow or underflow. Able to implement quantise/dequantise with per-tensor, per-channel and per-block scales and measure the error. Able to explain why loss scaling is needed and what signals it has gone wrong (NaNs, stalled loss). Able to read a convergence curve and decide whether a divergence is a numerics bug or a learning-rate problem. Practical experience: having quantised a real model and measured both the memory saved and the quality lost on a task you chose yourself.

**BUILDABLE** —
(a) FP8-Flow-MoE needs a 671B-parameter MoE training run on GPUs with FP8 matrix units: aggregate memory in the terabytes, disk in the terabytes for weights, optimiser state and checkpoints, days of wall clock, and a cost I will not invent. **Not reproducible here, and not partially either** — it is a convergence claim at a scale no single machine reaches.
(b) Smallest experiment, and it fits comfortably: quantise a small open model's weight and activation tensors in software — E4M3 and E5M2 by masking mantissa bits, then MX-style block scaling at 128, 32 and 16. Measure relative error per tensor and locate it in the distribution's tail. Then show double quantisation error directly: quantise row-wise, transpose, quantise column-wise, and the two scale sets disagree — the round trip is lossy. A few gigabytes, no accelerator. Separately, run the model at 4-bit and 16-bit locally and measure memory and tokens per second.
HARDWARE: one Mac suffices for the error and block-scaling experiments; FP8/FP4 hardware matrix paths are not exercisable on it, and the convergence result is out of reach.

## Reliability, failure and goodput in large-scale training infrastructure

**NAME** — Training reliability; fault tolerance and checkpointing; straggler and fail-slow detection; elasticity; goodput (ML Productivity Goodput, MFU).

**WHY IT MATTERS** — A synchronous training step finishes when its slowest participant finishes, and it restarts from the last checkpoint when any participant dies. On thousands of accelerators, both events are routine rather than exceptional, so the useful work a cluster delivers is not its peak FLOP/s but the fraction of wall clock spent making progress. This is the one subject in the area whose object is the infrastructure itself. It is live now because fail-slow behaviour — a node that is degraded but not dead — defeats the health checks the field had, and because operators have started publishing metrics that measure progress instead of utilisation.

**FOUNDATIONS** — No single canonical reference; the subject borrows from two older ones. Checkpoint/restart theory from high-performance computing, where the optimal checkpoint interval goes as the square root of (checkpoint cost × mean time between failures) — the Young/Daly result, which I did **not** fetch in this session and state here as recollection. And the goodput idea from networking: count only bytes, or steps, that survive. Named instrument in this area: ML Productivity Goodput (Wongpanich et al., MLSys 2026), which decomposes fleet efficiency into scheduling, runtime and program components. The constraint is structural rather than mathematical: synchronous collective communication makes every rank's progress the minimum over all ranks, so one slow rank taxes the whole job and one dead rank stops it.

**LIVE RESEARCH** — Open: detecting degradation before it costs a run, and recovering without restarting everything. Dated items from instrument B (MLSys 2026, May 2026). **GUARD** (<https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/339caf45a6fa281cae8adc6465343464-Abstract-Conference.html>) pairs lightweight in-training monitoring with an offline node sweep that qualifies nodes before they join production, explicitly because standard checks — NCCL tests, GPU burn-in — miss "long-running fail-slow behaviors"; it reports mean FLOPs utilisation up to 1.7× better and run-to-run step variance falling from 20% to 1%, and claims higher MTTF **with no figure given**, and no stated deployment size. **ML Productivity Goodput** (<https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/fbe2b2f74a2ece8070d8fb073717bda6-Abstract-Conference.html>, Google, production TPU fleet of "thousands of accelerators") argues utilisation-based metrics miss the real gaps and reports "scheduling goodput exceeding 95% for all job sizes through careful preemption tuning". Also at MLSys 2026: "Sparing Strategies to Minimize Reliability Impact On Large Training Jobs", GhostServe (shadow checkpointing for fault-tolerant serving), Hawkeye (reproducing GPU-level non-determinism). From instrument A, October 2026: "Leto: Fast In-Place Recovery for LLM Training on Surviving Hardware".

**TRANSITION** — Established engineering: periodic checkpointing and automatic job restart; this is assumed by every paper here rather than argued for. Becoming practice: goodput-style metrics replacing utilisation as the number operators manage against — one production fleet has published its decomposition, which is one operator, not a consensus. Still experimental: fail-slow detection and in-place recovery on surviving hardware. GUARD's own motivation is that the standard tools do not catch fail-slow, and Leto is an October 2026 preprint; the before/after numbers for both come from the originating groups with no independent replication in anything I read.

**ENGINEERING** — Built from three parts: a checkpoint path fast enough to run often (asynchronous, sharded, and limited by storage bandwidth), a health and performance monitor that compares each rank against its peers, and a scheduler that can drain, replace and resume. Real tools named in the sources: NCCL health tests and GPU burn-in as the incumbent checks that GUARD says are insufficient; vendor fleet tooling at Google. The central trade-off is checkpoint frequency against overhead — checkpoint too often and you spend the run writing, too rarely and a failure costs more recomputation than the writes would have — and the second is eviction policy: a node you suspect of being slow is also a node you paid for.

**SKILLS** — Able to derive the optimal checkpoint interval from checkpoint cost and failure rate, and to say how the answer moves as a cluster grows. Able to instrument a step and detect an outlier rank from timing alone, distinguishing a slow rank from a slow collective. Able to compute goodput for a real run: wall clock minus failed segments, restarts and checkpoint writes. Able to reason about the difference between a crash and degradation, and why the second is harder to see. Practical experience: having lost a long run and recovered it, and having written a checkpoint path that does not stall training while it writes.

**BUILDABLE** —
(a) Both headlines require the fleet: GUARD's claims concern clusters at tens of thousands of GPUs, the goodput paper's a production TPU fleet of thousands of accelerators. Memory, disk and time are the cluster's, not a machine's; the cost is a datacentre's operating budget, which I will not price. **Nothing here is reproducible on the machine, and no shrunken version would be the experiment** — the phenomenon is a property of scale.
(b) Smallest experiment, and it does fit, because the mechanism is probabilistic rather than physical. Simulate N ranks with exponential failure times, a checkpoint cost, and a step time equal to the maximum over ranks; sweep the interval, plot goodput, compare the optimum with the square-root rule. Then make one rank 10% slow and measure the whole-job penalty — why fail-slow beats failure as a problem. Then checkpoint a small local model to disk and measure write bandwidth and stall. Minutes of CPU, no API spend.
HARDWARE: one Mac supports the simulation and the checkpoint-bandwidth measurement; the published results require a cluster, which is itself the finding.
